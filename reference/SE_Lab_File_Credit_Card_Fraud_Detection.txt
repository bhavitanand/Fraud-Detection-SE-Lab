Experiment 1: Problem Statement
Aim:- To write down the problem statement for a suggested system of relevance: the Credit
Card Fraud Detection System.

Description
Credit card fraud is the unauthorised use of a payment card, or of its details, to obtain goods,
services or cash. As card-not-present (online) payments, digital wallets and contactless
payments have grown, fraudsters have more ways to steal card data through phishing,
skimming, data breaches and account takeover, and to use it before the real cardholder notices.
Banks and payment processors therefore need an automated way of deciding, within a fraction
of a second, whether a card transaction is genuine or suspicious.
A Credit Card Fraud Detection System is a software platform that receives every card
transaction as it happens, studies it against the cardholder's normal behaviour and known fraud
patterns, and assigns it a fraud risk score. Depending on that score the transaction is approved,
held for verification or declined, and suspicious cases are sent to a human fraud analyst for
review. The confirmed outcomes are then fed back so that the detection model keeps learning as
fraud tactics change.

Software Description
The proposed system is a web-based, service-oriented application. Its detection core is a
supervised machine-learning classifier (for example Random Forest or XGBoost) trained on
labelled historical transactions and combined with a configurable rule engine. It is exposed to
payment gateways through a REST API and to fraud analysts and administrators through a web
dashboard. A good fraud detection system should provide the following features:
   •   Real-time transaction scoring: every incoming transaction is scored within a few
       hundred milliseconds so that the payment flow is not delayed.
   •   Behavioural feature engineering: transaction velocity (number of transactions in the
       last minutes/hours), unusual amounts, new merchant or country, and distance or time
       between consecutive transactions.
   •   Hybrid detection: a machine-learning model to catch unseen patterns plus rules (for
       example, blocked countries or amount limits) for known fraud scenarios.
   •   Alert and case management: automatic alert creation, analyst assignment, review
       workflow and final verdict (fraud / false positive).
   •   Cardholder notification and step-up verification: SMS or e-mail alerts and
       confirmation of suspicious transactions by the cardholder.
   •   Continuous learning: confirmed fraud and false-positive labels are stored and used for
       scheduled model retraining and evaluation.
   •   Reports and dashboards: fraud rate, detection rate, false-positive rate, alert backlog
       and analyst productivity.

Problem in Existing System
   •   Class imbalance and hidden fraud: genuine transactions vastly outnumber fraudulent
       ones (in the widely used public ULB/Kaggle card dataset only 492 of 284,807
       transactions, about 0.17%, are fraud). Simple approaches therefore miss most fraud or
       generate too many false alarms.
   •   Rigid rule-based checks: static rules such as fixed amount limits are easily learned and
       bypassed by fraudsters, and they must be updated manually every time a new pattern
       appears.
   •   False positives: legitimate purchases that are wrongly declined annoy customers and
       cost the bank revenue and trust.
   •   Delayed detection: batch or manual review often finds fraud only after the money has
       already moved, when it can only be recovered through chargebacks.
   •   Concept drift: fraud tactics change quickly, so a model that is never retrained becomes
       less accurate over time.
   •   Data privacy constraints: real card data is sensitive and regulated, which makes it
       difficult to share data for building and testing detection systems.

Requirements
   •   Security and privacy: card numbers must be tokenised or masked, all traffic
       encrypted, and access limited by role in line with PCI DSS guidance.
   •   Low latency and high throughput: scoring must complete fast enough to sit inside the
       payment authorisation flow and must scale during peak shopping periods.
   •   Accuracy: high recall (fraud caught) while keeping precision reasonable so genuine
       customers are not blocked; evaluated with precision, recall, F1-score and PR-AUC
       instead of plain accuracy.
   •   Integration capability: standard REST/JSON interfaces so that the system can be
       plugged into existing payment gateways and core banking systems.
   •   Explainability: each alert should show the main reasons for the high score so that
       analysts can take a quick and justified decision.
   •   Auditability: every score, decision and analyst action must be logged for audits and
       dispute handling.
   •   Easy maintenance: modular services and scripted model retraining so that rules and
       models can be updated without downtime.

Cost Analysis
   •   The proposed technology stack (Python, FastAPI, scikit-learn/XGBoost, PostgreSQL,
       Redis and React) is open source, so there is no licence cost for the software itself; the
       main costs are hosting, storage, development effort and later maintenance.
   •   Commercial fraud-prevention products are usually priced per transaction or by
       subscription, which becomes expensive at high volumes. A custom system can be
       cheaper in the long run and can be tuned to an organisation's own data.
   •   The cost has to be weighed against the loss it prevents: direct fraud losses, chargeback
       and investigation fees, and the customer churn caused by both fraud and wrongly
       declined genuine payments.
   •   Automation lowers the manual review effort, because analysts only see the small
       fraction of transactions that the model marks as risky.
Limitation
   •   Data dependence: model quality depends on the quantity and quality of labelled data;
       confirmed fraud labels arrive late (after disputes/chargebacks).
   •   Trade-off between false positives and missed fraud: no threshold removes both;
       every setting accepts some errors.
   •   Adversarial behaviour: fraudsters adapt, so detection needs constant monitoring and
       retraining.
   •   Explainability of complex models: ensemble and deep models are harder to explain
       than simple rules.
   •   Security concerns: the system holds sensitive financial information, so a breach would
       be very costly; strong security controls are mandatory.

Future Scope
   •   Deep-learning and graph-based models that detect fraud rings by linking shared
       devices, addresses and merchants.
   •   Unsupervised and semi-supervised anomaly detection to spot completely new fraud
       patterns without labels.
   •   Behavioural biometrics and device fingerprinting as additional risk signals.
   •   Explainable AI techniques (such as SHAP values) to show analysts why a transaction
       was flagged.
   •   Federated learning so that banks can improve a shared model without exchanging raw
       customer data.
   •   Extension to other payment channels such as UPI, wallets and net banking.

Aim:- To do requirement analysis and develop the Software Requirement Specification sheet

1) Introduction
1.1) Purpose
This Software Requirements Specification (SRS) gives a detailed description of the Credit Card
Fraud Detection System to be developed. It states the functional and non-functional
requirements, the external interfaces and the constraints of the system, and it is intended for
developers, testers, project guides and the bank's fraud-operations staff. The primary purposes
of the system are:
   •     A) Detecting fraudulent transactions: scoring every card transaction in real time and
         classifying it as genuine, suspicious or fraudulent.
   •     B) Supporting fraud operations: assisting analysts in reviewing alerts, taking
         decisions and blocking compromised cards quickly.
   •     C) Reducing losses and false alarms: protecting cardholders and the issuing bank
         while keeping wrongly declined genuine transactions to a minimum.
1.2) Scope
The scope of the Credit Card Fraud Detection System includes:
   •     A) Transaction Ingestion: receiving transaction requests from a payment gateway
         through a secure API and validating them.
   •     B) Feature Engineering: building behavioural features from the transaction and the
         card's recent history.
   •     C) Fraud Scoring: combining a machine-learning model and a rule engine to produce a
         fraud risk score.
   •     D) Decision and Alerting: approving, holding or declining a transaction and creating
         alerts with cardholder notifications.
   •     E) Case Management: letting analysts review alerts, record verdicts and block or
         unblock cards.
   •     F) Model Management: training, evaluating and deploying new model versions from
         confirmed labels.
   •     G) Admin Panel and Reports: user and role management, rule/threshold
         configuration and performance reports.
The system does not perform the actual payment authorisation, clearing or settlement; it only
advises the authorisation flow with a risk decision.

Software Used:- StarUML / any UML-DFD drawing tool

Theory
Data Flow Diagrams (DFD) represent the flow of information through a system graphically
instead of describing it in text. A DFD shows external entities (sources and destinations of
data), processes (which transform data), data stores (where data rests) and data flows (labelled
arrows). DFDs are drawn at successively deeper levels:
   •   Level 0 DFD (context diagram): shows the whole system as a single process together
       with its external entities and the data exchanged with them.
   •   Level 1 DFD: breaks the single process into its major sub-processes and shows the data
       stores they use.
   •   Level 2 DFD: explodes one Level 1 process into more detailed sub-processes, with
       inputs, processing and outputs.
A Structure Chart is the function-oriented design counterpart of a DFD: it shows the module
hierarchy of the software, that is, which module calls which lower-level module.

Experiment 4: Entity Relationship (ER) Diagram
Aim:- To draw the ER diagram for the Credit Card Fraud Detection System.

Theory
An ER diagram is composed of entities, relationships, attributes and cardinality. Entities are the
things of interest about which data is stored, relationships are the associations between entities,
attributes describe the entities, and cardinality states how many instances of one entity can be
related to instances of another.
ER diagram components and features
   •   Entities: things of interest, such as Customer, Card or Transaction.
   •   Relationships: connections between entities, such as a Card making Transactions.
   •   Attributes: descriptors of entities, such as a transaction's amount.
   •   Cardinality: how many entities can be related (1:1, 1:N, M:N).
   •   Entity categories: strong, weak and associative entities.
   •   Entity keys: super, candidate and primary keys (PK); a foreign key (FK) links to the
       primary key of another entity.
   •   Attribute categories: simple, composite, derived, single-valued and multi-valued
       attributes.

   •   Generalization: a solid line with a hollow triangle showing that an actor or use case is a

Theory
A class diagram is a UML structural diagram that represents the structure of a system in terms
of classes and interfaces. It shows the attributes and operations of classes and the relationships
between them (association, generalization, dependency, etc.). Class diagrams map directly to
object-oriented languages. An object diagram is a snapshot of the system at a particular
moment: it shows the instances (objects) of classes with their attribute values and the links
between them. Object diagrams can be seen as instance-level class diagrams and are useful for
explaining small parts of a complex system.

Experiment 7: Behavioral View: State-Chart Diagram and Activity
Diagram
Aim:- To draw the state-chart diagram and the activity diagram for the Credit Card Fraud
Detection System.
Software Used:- StarUML

Theory
A state-chart (state machine) diagram describes the different states an object goes through
during its life and the events that cause it to move from one state to another. An activity diagram
shows the flow of control from one activity to another; it is like a flowchart with support for
decisions, parallel flows and swimlanes that show which actor or component performs each
activity.
State-Chart Diagram: Life Cycle of a Transaction




                          Fig. 7.1: State-chart diagram of a transaction

A transaction starts in the Received state. It is Rejected if the format or card checks fail;
otherwise it is Validated and moves to Scoring once features are computed. Depending on the
risk score it becomes Approved (low score), Held for Review (medium score) or Declined (high
score). Held and declined transactions go to Under Analyst Review, where the analyst marks
them Confirmed Fraud (card blocked, label stored) or Cleared as a false positive (transaction
released and approved). Approved transactions are finally Settled.

Activity Diagram (with Swimlanes)




                       Fig. 7.2: Activity diagram of the fraud detection process

The swimlanes show the activities of the Cardholder/Payment Gateway, the Fraud Detection
System and the Fraud Analyst. After a payment is initiated, the system validates it, fetches
history, computes features and scores it. A low score leads to approval; a medium or high score
leads to a decline/hold, an alert and a cardholder notification. The analyst reviews the alert and
either blocks the card (fraud) or releases the transaction (false positive), and the outcome is
added to the training data.

Theory
Sequence diagram: A sequence diagram is the most commonly used interaction diagram. It
depicts the interaction between objects in time order, that is, the order in which messages are
exchanged. It is also called an event diagram or event scenario and shows how, and in what
order, the objects of a system work together.
Collaboration diagram: A collaboration (communication) diagram shows the same
information as a sequence diagram but emphasises the relationships between objects instead of
time. The objects are drawn as nodes, linked to each other, and the messages between them are
numbered to show their order.
Sequence Diagram for the Fraud Detection System




                 Fig. 8.1: Sequence diagram of transaction scoring and alert review

The gateway submits a transaction to the Fraud API, which validates it, obtains features from
the Feature Store and the fraud probability from the ML Model, and applies the rules. The
transaction and decision are saved in the database. In the alternative flow (score above the high
threshold) an alert is created, the cardholder is notified and may confirm, and the DECLINE
decision is returned to the gateway. Later, the analyst fetches the open alerts and submits a
verdict, which the Alert Manager saves (and blocks the card if it is fraud).

A component diagram is used to break down a large object-oriented system into smaller
